# Reviewer Report

**Manuscript:** Beyond Static Responses: Multi-Agent LLM Systems as a New Paradigm for Social Science Research
**Journal:** Methods-oriented disciplinary social-science journal (demo run, *Political Analysis* mold)

**Recommendation:** Reject

---

## Summary

The manuscript proposes a six-tier taxonomy (Levels 0–5) of LLM-based systems for social-science research, ordered by functional thresholds — memory integration, goal-driven autonomy, planning and coordination, multi-agent coordination, and emergence — and mapped onto Boyd's OODA loop. Table 1 (p. 3) specifies each level's threshold criterion, required architecture, and key capability; Table 2 (pp. 6–7) sorts several dozen example systems from the literature into the six levels by application area. Sections 3.1–3.6 walk through the levels using published systems as illustrations. Section 4 argues that Levels 0–2 deliver efficiency gains while Levels 3–5 offer "epistemic value beyond automation" (p. 16), discusses reproducibility, validity, and ethical challenges, and sets out future directions. The paper presents no original data and self-describes as a "living document" (p. 1). The abstract claims the framework "clarifies the technical and methodological boundaries between different agentic architectures" and provides a "comprehensive overview of current capabilities" (p. 1).

## Major Concerns

### 1. The paper does not establish what it adds to the literature it cites

The contribution is never differentiated from prior work, including the paper's own sources. The manuscript cites at least eight surveys and reviews of the same terrain and uses every one purely as a content supplier — most tellingly, its own architectural account is derived from two of them: "The construction of these systems typically follows modular design patterns widely discussed in recent system surveys [Wang et al., 2024, Gao et al., 2024]" (p. 5). There is no related-work section and no sentence contrasting the six tiers with any prior framework; even the opening gap statement is supported by citations to two earlier works said to already make the same argument (p. 1). Beyond the surveys, the ladder recapitulates classical agent taxonomies without engaging them: Russell and Norvig's reflex/model-based/goal-based/learning agent progression, Wooldridge and Jennings's agency criteria, the BDI tradition, and the 0–5 levels-of-automation template exemplified by SAE J3016. The one device that would distinguish the scheme — the OODA overlay — stops discriminating exactly where the paper locates its frontier (Concern 2). As submitted, a reader cannot state what the framework adds, and the authors do not state it either.

### 2. The taxonomy contradicts its own defining thresholds

The scheme's load-bearing boundaries are affirmed and denied within the document. At Level 1, Table 1 gives the threshold "Memory Integration," the architecture "Session memory, prompt engineering," and the capability "Consistent persona or stateful behavior" (p. 3); Section 3.2 states that these same systems "remain stateless and externally controlled" and closes that they operate "without autonomous goal selection or memory" (p. 8). The criterion separating the first two rungs is thus both present and absent. At the top, Section 2.1 makes emergence the exclusive separator of the highest tier — "The difference between Level 4 and 5—as not visible in Figure 1—is the complexity of agentic systems on Level 5, which leads to emergent behavior based on the agents' interactions" (p. 4) — yet the Level 4 prose reports that "These multi-agent societies display emergent behaviors such as conformity, leadership, and conflict resolution" (p. 12). The OODA scaffold has the same problem: Section 2.1 claims "each level adds an OODA element" (p. 4), but the loop's four elements are exhausted at Level 3, and Table 1 labels Levels 4–5 "OODA + Learning" and "Dynamic OODA + Learning + Emergence" (p. 3) — additions beyond OODA, not elements of it. Table 1 further requires a "large-scale agent population" at Level 5 (p. 3) while Section 3.6 opens that tier with a platform it describes as "small-scale" (p. 13). These contradictions sit precisely at the boundaries the abstract claims to clarify.

### 3. No classification procedure exists, and the paper's own assignments are not reproducible

The framework is offered as a classification instrument — it "enables us to classify existing systems" (p. 2) — but no operational definitions, coding rules, or worked borderline cases appear anywhere, and the consequence is visible in the paper's own catalog. Table 2 (p. 6) assigns identical works to different tiers: Demszky et al. [2023] appears at Level 0 ("Qualitative coding") and Level 5 ("Emergent social dynamics"); Karjus [2025] appears at Level 0 ("Literature review") and Level 4 ("Scientific exploration") and is additionally invoked as evidence for Level 3 validity (p. 16); Mozikov et al. [2024] and Zhang et al. [2024a] each occupy Levels 1 and 2 simultaneously. A five-tier spread for one citation is not a borderline call. If the instrument's own authors cannot apply it consistently within a single table, two independent readers cannot be expected to converge — and demonstrated inter-coder agreement on systems the authors did not choose is the minimum a methods readership requires before a taxonomy counts as a usable instrument rather than a table of contents.

### 4. The validation claim is circular and the sample behind it is undisclosed

The paper's only validation statement cannot function as one. On p. 7 the examples are introduced as "not intended as an exhaustive or definitive list," and in the same paragraph the paper asserts that "These examples not only validate the proposed framework but also reveal the accelerating relevance of LLM agents." Examples selected by the authors to illustrate categories the authors defined cannot test those categories; no corpus sorted this way could fail to "validate" the scheme. No search strategy, database, date window, or inclusion criterion is stated anywhere, and the abstract's "comprehensive overview" (p. 1) is never reconciled with the body's "only a sample of current efforts" (p. 16). The table also mixes object types: review articles appear as tier exemplars alongside systems, and works cited elsewhere in the paper as evidence against the representational validity of persona simulation (pp. 8–9, 17) are listed as Level 1 exemplars of it (p. 6). The candor of the non-exhaustiveness disclaimer is to the authors' credit, but a disclaimer is not a selection method, and an illustration is not a validation.

### 5. The paper's distributional finding is contradicted by its own table

Section 4 claims that "higher levels that support coordination and emergence remain underexplored" (p. 16). Counting the citation instances actually listed in Table 2 (pp. 6–7) yields Level 0 = 12, Level 1 = 8, Level 2 = 13, Level 3 = 6, Level 4 = 19, Level 5 = 20. The companion claim that "Level 3 systems appear relatively scarce" (p. 16) is supported — six is the lowest count. But Levels 4 and 5 carry the highest citation density in the paper's own catalog, roughly 1.5 to 2.5 times the counts for Levels 0–2, which is arithmetic incompatible with "underexplored" as stated. Since Table 2 is the only evidence base the paper offers for either half of the sentence, the claim fails on the paper's own numbers — and given the undisclosed selection (Concern 4), neither half could in any case support an inference about the field's actual distribution.

### 6. The limitations are named with unusual candor but do not constrain the paper's claims

Section 4.2 names the strongest objections to the enterprise in the authors' own words — "there is no baseline to what to compare emergent phenomena to" and "The assumption that linguistic mimicry equates to psychological realism must therefore be treated with caution" (p. 17) — and this honesty is a genuine strength of the paper. But the first admission concedes that the criterion defining the top tier (Concern 2) is presently unmeasurable, and neither admission narrows any claim elsewhere. Section 4.2 closes: "Taken together, these challenges should not be mistaken as disqualifying the use of agentic systems in social science" (p. 17). The abstract retains "transformative potential" (p. 1), Section 4.1 assigns "epistemic value beyond automation" from Level 3 onward (p. 16), and the conclusion still offers "a conceptual and practical foundation" (p. 18). The result is that the tiers the paper values most are exactly the tiers it concedes cannot currently be validated, with no argument bridging the two positions. A limitation that never alters a claim has not been absorbed into the argument.

## Additional Concerns

- **Citation-key error with substantive consequences:** The reference list distinguishes Yang et al. [2024a] (PsychoGAT) from Yang et al. [2024b] (LLM-Measure, a text-measurement method) (p. 26), but Section 3.5 describes PsychoGAT and cites it as "[Yang et al., 2024b]" (pp. 12–13), and Section 4.1 cites Yang et al., 2024b in support of Level 4 claims about "inter-agent negotiation, group decision-making, norm formation" (p. 16), which the keyed work, a measurement method, cannot support.
- **Table 2 does not match the prose it summarizes:** Sakana AI's AI Scientist [Lu et al., 2024a] receives a full paragraph (p. 11) yet appears nowhere in Table 2, including its own "Research team" row; similar prose/table mismatches recur for other systems discussed at length. As a documentation-quality matter this undermines the table's function as the paper's summary device.
- **Hedged exemplars carried forward unhedged:** The paper concedes that several Level 3 examples "are not proper cases for social science" (p. 10) — a creditable hedge — but Table 2 lists them without caveat and the Discussion draws Level 3 validity conclusions from this tier (p. 16).
- **Capability evidence from outside the declared class:** A hybrid neuro-symbolic system and single-player game completions (sourced to consumer-technology journalism) are used to support claims about social coordination; the "Anecdotally" qualifier (p. 16) is present but the strong conclusion is drawn in the next sentence regardless.
- **No home for hybrid or hard cases:** Mixed human-AI systems are described (p. 12) and given a dedicated future-directions subsection, yet no tier assignment is ever offered for a hybrid, and no ambiguous case is discussed anywhere.
- **Classical ABM treated only as ancestry:** Agent-based modeling is never engaged as a live competitor with its own virtues (tractability, interpretability, seed-level reproducibility, cost), and the claim of "no baseline to what to compare emergent phenomena to" (p. 17) overlooks a two-decade literature on calibrating and validating ABMs against empirical patterns.
- **Precision and apparatus:** "over 99%" (p. 7) is anchored to no stated baseline; the citation base leans heavily on preprints for load-bearing claims; and Level 3 is named "Fully Agentic" in Table 1 (p. 3) but "LLM-based Agents" in Table 2 and the prose.

## Suggestions for Revision

The need the paper diagnoses is real — "LLM agent" genuinely spans architectures with radically different evidentiary properties — Table 2 has standing value as a bibliography, and the writing is clear. The following plan is offered in that spirit, but items 1–3 together would produce a substantially different manuscript rather than a revision of this one, which is what the recommendation reflects.

1. **Convert the catalog into a documented mapping review.** This is the single change that does the most work. A pre-specified search, stated inclusion and exclusion criteria, a written coding protocol keyed to checkable criteria, two independent coders, reported agreement, and a counted distribution across tiers would simultaneously repair Concerns 3, 4, and 5 — and would turn the paper's one latent empirical finding, the scarcity of Level 3 systems, into defensible methodological knowledge. Requires new analysis, not rewriting.
2. **Restate each threshold as a binary, inspectable criterion and resolve the internal contradictions** (Level 1 memory; the Level 4/5 emergence boundary, which likely means giving Level 5 an architectural criterion or merging Levels 4 and 5). This is coupled to item 1: no coding protocol can be written over thresholds the text both affirms and denies. Conceptual work plus rewriting.
3. **Add an explicit positioning section** stating what the six tiers offer that Wang et al. [2024], Gao et al. [2024], and the other cited surveys do not, and engaging the classical agency taxonomies (Russell and Norvig; Wooldridge and Jennings; the BDI tradition; the levels-of-automation template). Reading and rewriting only, but it determines whether a contribution exists to publish.
4. **Justify or drop the OODA overlay.** Either show what the loop discriminates above Level 3 or replace it; the BDI tradition maps more naturally onto the paper's memory/goal/plan thresholds and is the comparison a multi-agent-systems readership will expect.
5. **Align the claims language with the evidence:** "illustrate" for "validate"; a scoped statement for "comprehensive"; and carry Section 4.2's concessions into the abstract, Section 4.1, and the conclusion so that the limitations bind. Rewriting only.
6. **Repair the apparatus:** correct the Yang et al. [2024b] key and the p. 16 citation, reconcile Table 2 with the prose (including the missing AI Scientist entry), and unify the level names across Table 1, Table 2, and the text.
