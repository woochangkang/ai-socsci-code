# Agent 5 — The Situator

Manuscript: Haase & Pokutta, "Beyond Static Responses: Multi-Agent LLM Systems as a New Paradigm for Social Science Research" (arXiv:2506.01839v3 [cs.MA]).
Assessed as a CONCEPTUAL/FRAMEWORK + SURVEY submission to a methods-oriented disciplinary social-science journal (*Political Analysis* mold).
All quotes verified by grep against `manuscript.txt`; page numbers are PDF pages (verified against the page-break markers in the extracted text and spot-checked against the PDF).

- **Wang et al. [2024], "A survey on large language model based autonomous agents," *Frontiers of Computer Science*** — The manuscript uses it as a *source*, never as a comparator: it supplies the "modular design patterns widely discussed in recent system / surveys" (p. 5), the coordination-structure typology ("According to Wang et al. [2024], these coordination mechanisms can take various structural forms, ranging from centralized planning (via a root controller) to fully decentralized interaction paradigms," p. 5), the Level-2 architecture description (p. 9), and the Level-1 characterization in the Discussion (p. 16). — **Distinction claimed: none.** The manuscript never states what its six-tier scheme offers that this survey's own architectural decomposition does not. - **Gao et al. A reader cannot tell from the manuscript what is left for Haase & Pokutta to add.

- **Feng et al. A LLM digest for social scientist, 2023."). The single work the manuscript designates as the field's entry point for social scientists is unlocatable from the reference given. ## POTENTIALLY MISSING PRIOR WORKS

*Confidence is stated explicitly for each. All were checked against the manuscript's reference list and are absent.*

**A. Levels-of-autonomy schemes that a 0–5 continuum invites direct comparison to**

- **SAE J3016, levels of driving automation (Levels 0–5), SAE International** — *High confidence this standard exists in this form.* This is the canonical 0-through-5 autonomy ladder in engineering, and it is the structural template that virtually every "levels of AI autonomy" proposal since has either borrowed from or defined itself against. A six-tier 0–5 continuum in 2025 that does not mention it reads as unaware of its own genre.
- **Morris et al. (Google DeepMind), "Levels of AGI: Operationalizing Progress on the Path to AGI"** — *High confidence the paper exists with roughly this title and author line (Meredith Ringel Morris et al., 2023); moderate confidence on the publication venue (I believe an ML conference, possibly ICML 2024) — treat the venue as uncertain.* It proposes an explicit Level 0–5 scheme, separates *autonomy* from *capability* as orthogonal dimensions, and explicitly discusses the SAE analogy and the design criteria a levels framework should satisfy. This is the single most on-point uncited comparator, and it is in the manuscript's own citation neighbourhood (Morris is a co-author of Park et al. 2023, which *is* cited).
- **Sheridan & Verplank (1978) levels of automation; Parasuraman, Sheridan & Wickens, "A model for types and levels of human interaction with automation," *IEEE Transactions on Systems, Man, and Cybernetics*** — *High confidence both exist; moderate confidence on the exact year of the Parasuraman piece (I believe 2000).* This is the mature human-factors literature on graded autonomy, including the known critique that ordinal autonomy scales conflate distinct dimensions. The manuscript's framework is vulnerable to exactly that critique and never confronts it.
- **An industry five-stage progression (chatbots → reasoners → agents → innovators → organizations) attributed to OpenAI and widely reported in mid-2024** — *Moderate confidence in the reporting; low confidence in any citable primary source.* Mentioned only because a reviewer will notice the resemblance; not something I would require the authors to cite.

**B. Classical agent typologies the "functional thresholds" reinvent**

- **Russell & Norvig, *Artificial Intelligence: A Modern Approach*** — *High confidence.* The textbook agent ladder — simple reflex → model-based reflex (i.e. internal state/memory) → goal-based → utility-based → learning agent — is, tier for tier, the manuscript's Levels 0→1→2→3 plus learning. This is a taught undergraduate taxonomy. Proposing "memory integration, autonomy, planning and coordination, and adaptive learning" (p. 3) as novel functional thresholds without citing it is the most serious literature omission in the paper.
- **Wooldridge & Jennings, "Intelligent agents: theory and practice," *The Knowledge Engineering Review* (1995)** — *High confidence on authors, title, and journal; high confidence on the mid-1990s date.* The weak/strong agency distinction (autonomy, social ability, reactivity, pro-activeness) is the field-defining criterion set for "when is a program an agent," which is precisely the question Sections 2.1–2.2 pose.
- **Bratman, *Intention, Plans, and Practical Reason* (1987), and the BDI agent architecture (Rao & Georgeff, early 1990s)** — *High confidence BDI exists as the standard agent-architecture formalism; moderate confidence on the exact Rao & Georgeff venue/year, so I would phrase it as "the BDI tradition."* BDI decomposes agents into beliefs (memory/world model), desires (goals), and intentions (plans) — a far more natural formalism for the manuscript's memory/goal/planning thresholds than OODA, and the one a multi-agent-systems readership would expect. Its absence makes the OODA choice look unmotivated (see ISSUE 6).
- **Franklin & Graesser, "Is it an Agent, or just a Program? A Taxonomy for Autonomous Agents"** — *Good confidence it exists with this title and these authors, ~1996–97, in an Springer LNAI/ATAL volume; treat year and venue as uncertain.* An explicit taxonomy of autonomous agents.
- **Dennett, *The Intentional Stance* (1987, MIT Press)** — *High confidence.* Relevant because the manuscript repeatedly slides between "behaves as if it has goals" and "has goals" without a stance-taking move to license it.

**C. Framework papers for LLM agents specifically**

- **Sumers, Yao, Narasimhan & Griffiths, "Cognitive Architectures for Language Agents" (CoALA)** — *Good confidence on authors and title; I believe TMLR 2024, but treat the venue as uncertain.* This is a *framework* paper (not a survey) that organizes language agents by memory modules, action space (internal vs. external), and decision procedure — structurally the same job as the manuscript's Section 2.2, done earlier and more formally. It is the closest uncited competitor on the framework side.
- **Xi et al., "The Rise and Potential of Large Language Model Based Agents: A Survey" (2023)** — *Good confidence it exists as a very widely cited survey; uncertain about any journal version.* Uses a brain/perception/action decomposition and covers single-agent → multi-agent → agent society, which is the manuscript's own arc.
- **Guo et al., "Large Language Model based Multi-Agents: A Survey of Progress and Challenges" (IJCAI 2024)** — *Good confidence on title and that it appeared at IJCAI 2024; moderate.* A dedicated LLM-MAS survey — the manuscript's Level 4/5 territory.
- **A survey of LLM-agent-driven social simulation organized micro→macro (I associate it with a 2024 arXiv paper by Mou, Wei, Huang and colleagues, roughly "From Individual to Society")** — *Low-to-moderate confidence on the specific authorship; I flag this as "a survey of this exact shape almost certainly exists and should be located and engaged" rather than as a firm citation.*

**D. Silicon-sampling / LLM-as-participant validity literature and its critics**

The manuscript cites Argyle et al. 2023 (*Political Analysis*), Santurkar et al. 2023, Tjuatja et al. 2024, Rossi et al. 2024, Wang, Morgenstern & Dickerson 2025 (*Nature Machine Intelligence*), Qu & Wang 2024, and Sarstedt et al. 2024 — a reasonable start. Absent:

- **Bisbee, Clinton, Dorff, Kenkel & Larson, "Synthetic Replacements for Human Survey Data? The Perils of Large Language Models," *Political Analysis*** — *Good confidence on authors and journal; I believe 2024, treat the year as uncertain.* This is the target journal's own rebuttal to Argyle et al., showing instability and distributional distortion in LLM-generated survey responses. For a submission to a *Political Analysis*-mold venue, citing Argyle without Bisbee et al. is a conspicuous one-sidedness.
- **Dillion, Tandon, Gu & Gray, "Can AI language models replace human participants?" *Trends in Cognitive Sciences* (2023)** — *Good confidence on authors, title, and journal; year ~2023.*
- **Agnew et al., "The Illusion of Artificial Inclusion" (CHI 2024)** — *Moderate confidence on the venue/year; the argument (that synthetic participants displace rather than represent the people they stand in for) is one I am confident exists in the 2024 HCI literature.*
- **Harding, D'Alessandro, Laurence & Long, "AI language models cannot replace human research participants"** — *Low-to-moderate confidence on venue and year; phrase as uncertain.*
- **A "diminished diversity of thought" / distributional-collapse finding for LLM samples** — *I am confident this result class exists in the 2024 literature (I associate it with Park, Schoenegger & Zhu) but not confident enough in the citation to name a venue.* Directly relevant to the manuscript's Level 1 claim that persona conditioning yields "subgroup-like distributions" (p. 8).

**E. What a methods journal would additionally expect and does not find**

- **Gilardi, Alizadeh & Kubli, "ChatGPT outperforms crowd workers for text-annotation tasks," *PNAS* (2023)** — *High confidence.* The canonical reference for the manuscript's Level 0 claim that LLM coding rivals human coders (p. 7), which is currently supported only by Ziems et al. and Nilsson et al.
- **The design-based / imperfect-surrogate-label correction literature for LLM annotations (I associate this with Egami, Hinck, Stewart & Wei, NeurIPS 2023/2024)** — *Moderate confidence on authorship and venue; high confidence that a bias-correction literature for LLM-generated labels exists.* This is exactly the machinery a *Political Analysis* readership would expect before accepting "LLMs can replace coders."
- **ABM calibration/validation methodology — pattern-oriented modelling (Grimm et al., *Science*, ~2005), indirect inference, approximate Bayesian computation** — *High confidence this methodological tradition exists; moderate confidence on the Grimm et al. year.* Section 4.2 states there is "no baseline to what to compare emergent phenomena to" (p. 17). That is not true of the ABM literature, which has spent two decades on exactly this problem. Asserting the absence of validation standards without citing the existing ones is the clearest sign that the paper is not written from inside social-science methodology.

## CLAIMED GAP

The paper never states a gap in the negative form ("no existing framework does X"). The four framing sentences are:

> "We argue that the next major step in computational social science lies in moving beyond these static applica- / tions toward a systematic understanding of LLM-based agentic systems [Feng et al., 2025, Grossmann et al., 2023]." — **p. 1**

> "Building on this foundation, we propose a six-tier framework that captures the increasing complexity / and autonomy of LLM-based systems in social science research." — **p. 2**

> "Grounding this structure in functional thresholds and aligning it with the / OODA loop enables us to classify existing systems and clarify the developmental logic behind building LLM-based / simulations. ... this paper aims to lay a possible conceptual foundation / for the next generation of computational social science." — **p. 2**

> "By clarifying these developmental stages and their architectural dependencies—each aligned with OODA loop / phases—the framework that we propose contributes to a more nuanced understanding of agentic potential in / LLM-based systems." — **p. 5**

And retrospectively:

> "This paper set out to provide a conceptual scaffold for understanding and categorizing LLM-based agents in the / social sciences. ... The goal was not to catalog all existing systems but to distill the structural differences that / matter most for empirical, theoretical, and ethical engagement with agentic AI." — **p. 16**

## GAP VERDICT

**Does not hold.** The only explicit gap statement (p. 1) is supported by citations to two prior works that already make that argument, and the paper's other positioning claims ("a more nuanced understanding," "a possible conceptual foundation") are unfalsifiable comparatives with no stated comparator — while the eight-plus surveys and reviews of the same terrain that the paper itself cites are used exclusively as content suppliers and never once distinguished from.

## NOVEL CONTRIBUTION (one sentence)

**I cannot honestly produce a sentence stating what this manuscript adds to the literature.** The nearest candidate — a six-label ordinal vocabulary (LLM-as-Tool / LLM-as-Role / Agent-like / Fully Agentic / Multi-Agent / Complex Adaptive) overlaid on Boyd's OODA phases, plus a table sorting ~90 studies into those labels — restates an ordering (stateless → persona → goal-directed → tool-using → multi-agent → population-scale) that is already the standard organizing arc of the surveys the paper cites and already the structure of Russell & Norvig's textbook agent ladder; the labels and the OODA overlay are new packaging rather than new knowledge, and neither is validated, differentiated, nor applied consistently by the authors themselves.

There *is* one latent claim in the paper that would constitute a genuine contribution if it were done properly: the observation that "Level 3 systems appear relatively scarce" (p. 16) — an empirical claim about the shape of the field. As written it is asserted from an admittedly non-systematic table ("not intended as an exhaustive or definitive list," p. 7). Converted into a documented mapping review — pre-specified search, stated inclusion criteria, a written coding protocol, two coders, reported agreement, and a counted distribution across tiers with the gap tested rather than eyeballed — that single claim would be publishable methodological knowledge. That is the constructive path, and it is the reason my recommendation is not a flat reject.

## CONTRIBUTION TYPE

**Incremental, bordering on redundant.** Redundant with respect to the synthesis (the cited surveys already cover this ground, one of them in a social-science journal); incremental with respect to the framework (a relabeling of a standard ordering, with an added OODA overlay that does no discriminating work above Level 3).

## VENUE FIT

Poor as currently framed. The paper is written *about* social scientists rather than *for* them: it explains LLM architecture to a social-science audience but never engages the methodological apparatus that audience adjudicates with — no estimand, no identification logic, no measurement model, no validation design, no reliability reporting, and no engagement with the ABM calibration literature it declares nonexistent ("there is no baseline to what to compare emergent phenomena to," p. 17). The citation base tells the same story: of 126 references, 52 are arXiv preprints, roughly ten sit in core disciplinary social-science outlets, and the paper's evidence for state-of-the-art agentic capability is two consumer-technology news stories ("Anthropic's Claude 3.7 Sonnet achieved full game completion in February 2025 [Peter, 2025], followed by Google AI's Gemini 2.5," pp. 16–17, citing *The Verge* and *TechRadar*). A journal in the *Political Analysis* mold publishes methods whose properties are demonstrated — and it has already published the closest thing to this paper's subject (Argyle et al. 2023), a paper that earned its place by proposing a testable criterion and benchmarking it against real survey data. This manuscript cites that paper but adopts none of that discipline, and misfiles it at Level 2 (pp. 2, 6) despite Argyle et al. being single-shot prompt conditioning with no memory, planning, or task decomposition — a Level 0/1 system under the manuscript's own criteria.

## ISSUES

**ISSUE 1 — The paper never differentiates itself from the survey literature it cites**
**SEVERITY: Critical**
**DESCRIPTION:** At least eight reviews/surveys of overlapping scope are cited (Wang et al. 2024; Gao et al. 2024; Ziems et al. 2024; Thapa et al. 2025; Hou & Huang 2025; Lu et al. 2024b; Ke et al. 2024; Larooij & Törnberg 2025), and every one is used only to supply content or fill a Table 2 cell. The clearest instance: "The construction of these systems typically follows modular design patterns widely discussed in recent system / surveys [Wang et al., 2024, Gao et al., 2024]" (p. 5) — the paper's own architectural section is explicitly derived from the comparators. Gao et al. 2024 is a survey of LLM-empowered agent-based modelling in *Humanities and Social Sciences Communications*: same object, same audience, peer-reviewed. There is no related-work section, and the strings "taxonomy," "typology," "related work," "to our knowledge," and "unlike existing" appear nowhere in a positioning sense. A framework paper that cannot say what it adds to the frameworks it cites has not established a contribution.
**AFFECTED CLAIMS:** The entire contribution claim — abstract ("introduces a structured framework"), p. 2 ("we propose a six-tier framework"; "lay a possible conceptual foundation"), p. 5 ("the framework that we propose contributes to a more nuanced understanding"), p. 16 ("a conceptual scaffold").

**ISSUE 2 — The tiers do not discriminate: identical works are assigned to non-adjacent levels**
**SEVERITY: Critical**
**DESCRIPTION:** On a single page of Table 2 (p. 6), Demszky et al. [2023] — a *Nature Reviews Psychology* review article — appears both as a **Level 0** exemplar ("Qualitative coding: Ziems et al. [2024], Nilsson et al. [2025], Ke et al. [2024], Demszky et al. [2023]") and as a **Level 5** exemplar ("Emergent social dynamics: ... Wang et al. [2025b], Demszky et al. [2023]"). Karjus [2025] appears at **Level 0** ("Literature review," p. 6), at **Level 4** ("Scientific exploration," p. 6), and is invoked in the Discussion as evidence for **Level 3** validity (p. 16) — one paper occupying three tiers. Mozikov et al. [2024] and Zhang et al. [2024a] each occupy Levels 1 and 2 simultaneously (p. 6). Sakana's AI Scientist (Lu et al. 2024a) is discussed as a **Level 4** "Research Team" (p. 11) while its successor AI Scientist-v2 (Yamada et al. 2025) is tabled at **Level 3** (p. 6). PaperBench is tabled at **Level 3** yet described in the text as a system that "simulates a team of agents, each assuming a distinct scientific role (e.g., PhD student, reviewer, PI)" (p. 10) — a multi-agent system by the framework's own Level 4 definition. Argyle et al. [2023], single-shot prompt conditioning, is placed at Level 2 and cited for "structured autonomy, integrating task-oriented reasoning and memory architectures that enable multi-step planning" (p. 2). An ordinal scheme whose own authors cannot apply it reproducibly has no demonstrated analytic value; this is the empirical test of the framework's worth, and it fails.
**AFFECTED CLAIMS:** "Grounding this structure in functional thresholds ... enables us to classify existing systems" (p. 2); "These examples not only validate the proposed framework" (p. 7); the entirety of Table 2; the Level-3-scarcity claim (p. 16), which is computed off these assignments.

**ISSUE 3 — No documented method for the synthesis; the survey component is unauditable**
**SEVERITY: Major**
**DESCRIPTION:** The paper offers no search strategy, no databases, no date window, no inclusion or exclusion criteria, no coding protocol, and no reliability check — the words "PRISMA," "systematic review," "inclusion criteria," and "search" (in this sense) do not appear. The authors disclaim exhaustiveness ("Crucially, the examples provided in this paper, summarized in Table 2, are not intended as an exhaustive or definitive list," p. 7) but then draw an inference that requires a sampling frame: "Level 3 systems appear relatively scarce" (p. 16). You cannot infer scarcity from a convenience sample you have declared non-exhaustive. For a methods journal, an unreproducible synthesis is not a synthesis.
**AFFECTED CLAIMS:** Table 2 in full; "Level 3 systems appear relatively scarce, likely due to the overhead of maintaining coherent individual agents" (p. 16); "some tiers, especially Levels 0 to 2, are already being widely implemented ... higher levels ... remain underexplored" (p. 16).

**ISSUE 4 — Level 1 is defined by memory and simultaneously defined as memoryless**
**SEVERITY: Major**
**DESCRIPTION:** Table 1 (p. 3) gives Level 1's Threshold Criterion as "Memory Integration" and its Required Architecture as "Session memory, prompt engineering." The Introduction agrees: Level 1 systems "introduce basic state retention ... This level adds a layer of memory, allowing agents to maintain contextual awareness over short dialogue sequences" (p. 2). Section 3.2 then states the opposite twice: "These systems remain stateless and externally controlled" (p. 8) and "In sum, Level 1 systems simulate role-specific behavior without autonomous goal selection or memory" (p. 8). Memory is the *defining* threshold separating Level 0 from Level 1; if the paper cannot hold that distinction fixed across seven pages, the continuum has no operational content at its first step.
**AFFECTED CLAIMS:** Table 1; the "functional thresholds" claim (p. 3); the OODA mapping of Level 1 to "Observe → Act" (p. 3), which is licensed only by the presence of memory.

**ISSUE 5 — The Level 5 threshold is a behavioral outcome, not an architectural property, so the framework cannot perform the classification it promises**
**SEVERITY: Major**
**DESCRIPTION:** Table 1 (p. 3) lists Level 5's Threshold Criterion as "Emergence + Adaptation." Levels 1–4 are defined by inspectable design features (session memory; task objectives and control logic; memory store, tool use, API access; orchestration layer and communication protocols). Emergence is not a design feature — it is something a system may or may not exhibit when run, and whether it counts as emergent is itself contested. The paper concedes it has no criterion: "The difference between Level 4 and 5—as not visible in Figure 1—is the complexity of agentic systems on Level 5, which leads to emergent behavior based on the agents' interactions" (p. 4), which defines the top tier by "complexity" and then by the outcome that complexity supposedly produces. The framework therefore cannot do what it claims — "classify existing systems" (p. 2) — at precisely the tier where the paper locates its novelty. Section 4.2 compounds this by admitting "there is no baseline to what to compare emergent phenomena to" (p. 17), which concedes the top tier is not only unclassifiable but unfalsifiable.
**AFFECTED CLAIMS:** Table 1 Level 5 row; the 4-vs-5 distinction throughout Section 3.6; "enables us to classify existing systems" (p. 2); the abstract's claim to clarify "the technical and methodological boundaries between different agentic architectures."

**ISSUE 6 — The OODA overlay, the framework's one distinctive move, does no discriminating work and is adopted without justification against standard alternatives**
**SEVERITY: Major**
**DESCRIPTION:** OODA is the only element that visibly distinguishes this scheme from the standard survey ordering, so it must carry the contribution. It does not. Table 1 (p. 3) exhausts the four OODA phases at Level 3 ("Observe → Orient → Decide → Act (OODA)"); Levels 4 and 5 are then defined by "OODA + Learning" and "Dynamic OODA + Learning + Emergence" — i.e. by two non-OODA additions. The lens therefore contributes nothing exactly where the paper claims its novelty (multi-agent and complex adaptive systems). The lower-tier mapping is also arbitrary: a persona-prompted model does not "observe" more than a stateless one, and both receive an input. Separately, the paper calls Boyd's loop "a decision-theoretic lens" (p. 2) — OODA is a military strategic heuristic, not a decision theory, and the two supporting citations are a posthumous compilation of Boyd's briefings (Boyd 2018) and a monograph about Boyd (Osinga 2007). No justification is offered for choosing OODA over BDI (beliefs/desires/intentions), the standard multi-agent-systems formalism, which maps far more naturally onto the paper's own memory/goal/plan thresholds and is uncited.
**AFFECTED CLAIMS:** "These thresholds align with the OODA loop ... providing a decision-theoretic lens" (p. 2); "aligning it with the OODA loop enables us to classify existing systems" (p. 2); "each aligned with OODA loop phases—the framework that we propose contributes to a more nuanced understanding" (p. 5); Table 1's OODA Phase column; Figure 1.

**ISSUE 7 — No engagement with the pre-existing taxonomies of agency and autonomy that the six tiers reproduce**
**SEVERITY: Major**
**DESCRIPTION:** The paper proposes "memory integration, autonomy, planning and coordination, and adaptive learning" as its functional thresholds (p. 3) and derives a 0–5 ladder from them, without citing: Russell & Norvig's textbook agent ladder (reflex → model-based/internal state → goal-based → utility-based → learning), which is the same ladder; Wooldridge & Jennings (1995) on weak vs. strong agency, the field-defining answer to "when is a program an agent"; the BDI tradition (Bratman 1987; Rao & Georgeff), which formalizes exactly memory/goal/plan; Franklin & Graesser's agent taxonomy; the human-factors levels-of-automation literature (Sheridan & Verplank; Parasuraman, Sheridan & Wickens), which contains the standard critique that ordinal autonomy scales conflate independent dimensions; SAE J3016, the structural template for 0–5 autonomy ladders; and Morris et al.'s "Levels of AGI," which separates autonomy from capability and states design desiderata for levels frameworks. I have stated my confidence for each of these above; the high-confidence items (Russell & Norvig, Wooldridge & Jennings, BDI, SAE) are alone sufficient to make the point. Notably, the authors cite their own prior levels framework (Haase & Pokutta 2024, "Levels of Creative Collaboration") without relating the two schemes. A reviewer's unavoidable inference is that a six-tier autonomy continuum was constructed without consulting the literature on constructing autonomy continua.
**AFFECTED CLAIMS:** "we propose a continuum structured by a series of functional thresholds" (p. 3); the novelty of Table 1; the abstract's "structured framework."

**ISSUE 8 — Venue, register, and the "living document" self-description are incompatible with an archival journal record**
**SEVERITY: Major**
**DESCRIPTION:** The paper states: "this paper is intended as a 'living document' that summarizes current trends and approaches. We plan to update it regularly" (p. 1), and instructs readers to cite a URL rather than a fixed version: "If you need a stable identifier for the latest version of this paper please use the arxiv base url, e.g., https://arxiv.org/abs/2505.xxxx" (p. 1) — a placeholder that is both unfilled and the wrong month (the actual identifier is 2506.01839). The dateline reads "June 2, 2025" while the version stamp reads 18 May 2026, so the document's own metadata has already drifted. A journal article is a version of record with a DOI; a paper whose authors state that the published version will be superseded and who direct readers elsewhere for the current text is asking the journal to archive something the authors have declared provisional. The shelf-life problem is concrete rather than hypothetical: 52 of 126 references are arXiv preprints, the frontier evidence is two consumer-tech news articles dated February and May 2025 (*The Verge*; *TechRadar*, pp. 16–17), and a methods journal's typical lag would put publication a year or more after those claims went stale. Combined with the citation profile (roughly ten core social-science venues out of 126) and the absence of any social-science methodological apparatus, the manuscript reads as a cs.MA landscape document rather than a contribution to social-science methodology.
**AFFECTED CLAIMS:** The suitability of the entire manuscript for archival publication; the currency of Section 3 and Table 2; the "note to the reader" (p. 1).

---

### Summary judgement for the editor

The framework is clean and the writing is clear, and Table 2 has real value as a pointer list. But on the question this assessment exists to answer — does the manuscript advance on existing work? — the answer is no as currently constituted. The paper cites at least eight reviews of its own terrain and differentiates itself from none of them; its six tiers reproduce a textbook agent ladder without citing it; its one distinctive device (OODA) stops discriminating above Level 3; and its own authors assign the same works to non-adjacent tiers, including one review article placed at Levels 0 and 5 on the same page. There is a real paper latent here — a documented, coded, counted mapping review that tests the "missing Level 3" hypothesis and positions itself explicitly against Gao et al. 2024, Wang et al. 2024, and Larooij & Törnberg 2025 — but that paper would need to be written. **Recommendation from this station: reject as a framework contribution; invite resubmission only if reframed as a systematic mapping review with a documented protocol and an explicit positioning section.**
